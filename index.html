OpenAI launches Astra, its powerful (and controversial) new model
TechCrunch · September 3, 2026
Key takeaway
Greg Brockman called Astra OpenAI's “most intelligent and, also very importantly, our most aligned model yet,” and it shipped first to Daybreak cybersecurity customers before reaching Pro, Plus, Enterprise and API. The controversy is the part PMs should actually read: Astra uses opaque recurrence, a reasoning technique that obscures chain of thought — the very trace researchers use to audit why a model decided what it decided. The mental model shift: capability and auditability have started trading off against each other, and that tradeoff now lands on your desk as a product decision. For any feature where you'd have to explain a model's reasoning to a customer, a regulator, or your own risk team, the most capable model is no longer automatically the right one.

Must read
How top PMs increase their leverage with AI
Lenny's Newsletter (Colin Matthews) · June 30, 2026
Key takeaway
The cleanest framework anyone has published on this: three ladders, each a different kind of leverage. Personal leverage clears your own to-do list, product leverage ships the right things faster, and systems leverage builds repeatable steps so work can be outsourced to AI consistently. Each rung is an order of magnitude more leverage than the last — rung one is AI assisting your work, rung two is passing tasks over and reviewing output, rung three is AI completing multi-step tasks and checking its own results. The concrete thing to do this week is diagnose yourself honestly: most PMs live on rung one across all three ladders and mistake that for being AI-native. Pick the single ladder where you're highest and push one rung, because the systems ladder is the only one that compounds while you sleep.

Lenny's Newsletter · July 7, 2026
Key takeaway
The survey's sharpest finding is a gap, not a number: 64% of tech workers feel “excited” but only 33% feel “hopeful” — excitement about the present running well ahead of hope about where it leads. The workforce has split into four types along the AI fault line, with The Energized (41%) the least burned out and the only segment positive on the field's future, while the rest are more burned out and less optimistic than a year ago. Thompson's weekly round-up centres on a deceptively basic product question: does the world need a foldable iPhone at $2,000–$3,200? I Vibe Coded a Security Risk
Every · Working Overtime (Katie Parrott) · August 10, 2026, updated September 8, 2026
Key takeaway
Parrott shipped an agent connector for her own app, asked Claude to “do the safe version,” tested that it worked, and deployed. A later review by a newer model found a public registration route — an open door — live in production. Her diagnosis is the non-obvious part and it generalises well past code: not hubris but the illusion of explanatory depth. Because she could read the agent's chain of thought and the words made sense, she felt she understood work she had not done, and “it works when I click it” became evidence of done-ness. She also names the underlying pattern from OpenAI's own data — task crossover, using AI to do work belonging to another occupation, present in 16.8% of 800,000 work messages. Internalize her rule: if AI built the thing, that same system's reassurance cannot be the only evidence it's ready. Every PM now ships across boundaries they can't personally audit, and testing the happy path is not testing.

Compound Engineering: The Definitive Guide
Every · Source Code (Kieran Klaassen) · July 12, 2026
Key takeaway
The core claim is a standard worth stealing: each unit of work should make the next unit easier, not harder — most people treat AI as an extra pair of hands, compound engineering treats it as a team that sharpens with every task. It now ships as a zero-config plugin with 26 specialized agents, 23 workflow commands and 13 skills, and the loop expanded from four steps to seven, with ideate and plan moved to the front and polish to the end, precisely because AI now handles the middle. That reordering is the insight for PMs. As execution compresses, the work that differentiates you migrates to the two ends — framing the problem well at the start, and judging quality at the finish. Apply this principle to your own process before your next cycle: name what you're doing at the front and the back, because the middle is no longer where your value shows up.
