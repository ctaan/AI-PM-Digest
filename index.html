Key takeaway
Astra is API-priced identically to Claude Fable 5 and 5.1 — $10/million input and $50/million output — and scores 99.9% on the ARC‑AGI 3 benchmark. The mental model shift: a benchmark number describes a model plus a harness, never a model alone. Apply this by asking any AI vendor which harness produced their headline number, and whether you get that harness.

Must read
Write Things Down
Stratechery (Ben Thompson) · September 8, 2026
Key takeaway
Thompson's claim is that writing things down is the mechanism behind both human progress and current AI capability — and he backs it with a build worth copying. Having started more side projects than he could track, he created an agent that kept a record of what he was actively working on, what was waiting for him, and a growing queue, then had it generate a status board, then rebuilt the whole thing deterministically and realised “I basically wrote a harness, one which I intend to scale to everything I do and everyone I work with.” He also declines to call Astra AGI, on the grounds that his definition is AI that learns continuously, which frozen-weight models do not. The workflow to build this week is that status board: put your own open loops in files an agent maintains rather than in your head. And internalise his closing line — writing things down is powerful, but “its power will always pale in comparison to getting things done.”

Must read
Autonomy and Innovation
Stratechery (Ben Thompson) · August 24, 2026
Key takeaway
The asymmetry at the centre of this piece is unusually clean: an attacker's automated attempt has positive expected value, because a failed exploit leaves the status quo untouched and a successful one wins everything; a defender's automation has negative expected value, because a successful patch merely preserves the status quo while a bad one breaks the software. Thompson then applies the same math to companies — incumbents approach AI “with a bias towards a negative expected value framing” and keep humans in the loop as a bottleneck, while “for startups the base case is failure,” so leaning fully into AI is all upside. The framing PMs at established companies should adopt: your organisation's caution is structural rather than a failure of nerve — and it is precisely the gap a smaller competitor is built to exploit. Use it to argue for one ring-fenced surface where your team is allowed to operate at startup risk tolerance.

Comment: There's No Limit to How Bad Code Can Get
Simon Willison · September 6, 2026
Key takeaway
Willison's walk-through of the from-scratch rewrite is the most useful thing a PM can read before a replatform conversation. The old system stays a moving target because it runs the core business; its maintainers stop investing because it is about to be obsolete, so technical debt keeps mounting; the new team starts fast but discovers “nobody fully understands the behavior and scope of the thing they are replacing” — and you finish with two systems where you used to have one. His recommendation instead: shore up the old system with as much automated testing as possible, then see whether targeted refactors can get it to the desired shape (he points to Will Larson's “Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt”). Internalise this now, because agents make greenfield feel cheap while leaving the coordination math exactly as bad.
