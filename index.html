PM Thought Leaders

Must read
Autonomy and Innovation
Stratechery (Ben Thompson) · August 24, 2026
Key takeaway
Thompson's sharpest argument in months: automated attackers have positive expected value from every attempt, while automated defenders have negative expected value — a successful patch merely preserves the status quo, and one bad patch breaks production. That asymmetry, he argues, is the real reason AI diffusion is slow: incumbents evaluate AI through a “what could blow up in our faces” lens, so they keep humans in the loop and stay bottlenecked, which is why the genuinely AI-native companies will be startups rather than incumbents overhauling how they work. The mental model shift for a senior PM at an established company: your organisation's caution is structural, not a failure of will, so the leverage is in finding the workflows where a mistake is cheap and reversible and automating those end to end — partial automation just moves the bottleneck.

Applied AI Workflows for PMs

Must read
AI Is Ready. Organizations Aren't.
Every (Natalia Quintero) · June 29, 2026
Key takeaway
Natalia Quintero, who runs Every Consulting, makes the case that AI adoption isn't being held back by the models but by the organisation, and lays out a five-step framework as a concrete 60-day plan. The number that should stick: of nearly 2,000 organisations surveyed, only 6% met the criteria for success — and those companies were roughly three times as likely to have fundamentally redesigned their workflows, not just bought tools. She also names a specific failure pattern worth watching for: leaders making planning decisions about AI without ever having worked directly with it on sophisticated tasks. This is the most actionable thing here this week — pick one workflow you own, redesign it around what an agent can actually do, and treat the tool purchase as the last step rather than the first.

Must read
Don't classify. Hallucinate!
Simon Willison · August 14, 2026
Key takeaway
Faced with 1,856 tags on his own blog — far too many to fit in a prompt — Willison highlights Doug Turnbull's inversion: don't hand the model your taxonomy and ask it to classify. Let it invent the tags it thinks apply, then use vector embeddings to snap each invented tag to the closest real one. It's a genuinely non-obvious technique with wide application, and it's the concrete thing to build this week: any PM sitting on a large messy taxonomy — support tickets, feature requests, churn reasons, partner categories — can run this pattern over a backlog of free-text feedback and get usable structure without maintaining a prompt-sized label list. The mental model shift: constrain the model's output after generation, not before.
